# Squirrels, Nut Trees, Seasons and Wind for Furious Squirrels

Both squirrel families are real residents of Hot Springs National Park. The NPS species database lists the eastern gray squirrel as **Present** and the eastern fox squirrel as **Probably Present**, with the southern flying squirrel also **Present**; research-grade iNaturalist photos put fox squirrels inside the park in 2020 and 2024. Grays are the slimmer, white-bellied, closed-forest squirrel. Fox squirrels are heavier and rusty-bellied, and favor open ridges, edges and scattered trees. Both bury nuts in fall, live in leaf nests and tree cavities, breed in a December–January rut and again in late spring, and scold predators with barks and tail flags. The park's own plant list confirms nearly every mast tree the game needs: white, northern red, southern red, black, post and blackjack oaks; mockernut, black, bitternut and (probably) shagbark and pignut hickories; probably pecan, black walnut, American hazelnut and American beech; Ozark chinquapin (Present); sweetgum; shortleaf pine; and probably Osage-orange. Almost all of their nuts drop in **September–November**, which is also Arkansas's main squirrel-feeding season. Fall color in the Ouachitas peaks around **early November**. Ten years of hourly airport data (2016–2025, computed for this file) show the Hot Springs wind is **light**, about 4–6 mph on average, calm about a quarter of the time, and **most often from the east** in every season.

Era: all scenes are effectively `modern` (2000–2026) wildlife; the species and trees are native, so they also fit any earlier era except where noted (Ozark chinquapin was common before about 1940–1960, then blight-reduced).

### Coat colors (for character art)

Written descriptions:

- **Gray squirrel:** back and sides gray, "rarely reddish or all black"; "the fringe of the tail and belly are white." Black individuals can occur in litters, ranging from glossy black to shades between black and gray; albinos occur occasionally — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel). Gray coats; "Northern individuals may be black" — [UA Extension](https://uaex.uada.edu/environment-nature/musings-on-nature-blog/posts/squirrels.aspx).
- **Fox squirrel:** back and sides reddish yellow mixed with gray; tail fringe and belly reddish yellow; rarely all black. Black or albino fox squirrels are rare in Missouri — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel). Coat matches the red fox, hence the name — [UA Extension](https://uaex.uada.edu/environment-nature/musings-on-nature-blog/posts/squirrels.aspx). The widespread western subspecies *S. n. rufiventer* has an "orange to rust-colored venter" (via search summary) — [Plazi species account](https://treatment.plazi.org/GgServer/html/064D0660FFEEED14FAF2FEE6FD99FF32). A 1939 museum specimen from northern Arkansas is labeled *rufiventer* (via search summary) — [iDigBio record](https://www.idigbio.org/portal/records/44bdcbfd-a006-4d45-8c3e-fdfeedb34e67).
- **Dark-face variants:** a black facial mask with white nose, paws and ear tips is documented for **southeastern** fox squirrels: South Carolina's 2022 sighting survey was 67% gray phase, 31% black and 2% brown, and Mississippi's hill-country form *S. n. bachmani* has a black mask and white nose and ears (via search summary) — [SCDNR: Southern Fox Squirrel](https://www.dnr.sc.gov/wildlife/species/foxsquirrel.html); [MDWFP: Mississippi squirrel species](https://www.mdwfp.com/wildlife-hunting/squirrel-program/squirrel-management-info/ms-squirrel-species/). **[UNVERIFIED]** for Arkansas: no source found that the masked phase occurs in Garland County. Use it at most as an optional "elder" or rare skin.
- **Flying squirrel:** "mousy gray" with especially large black eyes — [UA Extension](https://uaex.uada.edu/environment-nature/musings-on-nature-blog/posts/squirrels.aspx).

### Habitat preference

- **Grays:** "bottomlands and rivers with a bushy understory" — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel). Gray squirrels are "likely to found in more densely wooded areas" (via search summary) — [hunter-ed.com Arkansas study guide](https://www.hunter-ed.com/arkansas/studyGuide/Small-Game-Squirrel/201003_116846). Grays prefer dense, closed forest (via search summary, low-quality aggregator) — [Outforia](https://outforia.com/species/fox-squirrel/in-ar).
- **Fox squirrels:** "higher ridges," Osage-orange hedge fences, farm woodlots, timbered fence rows and draws — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel). They favor "open areas with widely spaced trees" and are often seen in city parks — [UA Extension](https://uaex.uada.edu/environment-nature/musings-on-nature-blog/posts/squirrels.aspx).
- **Both:** urban areas, "especially with large oak and hickory trees" — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel).
- Arkansas context: a search summary quoted an AGFC source saying bottomlands "tend to produce healthier populations of squirrels" because they produce more hard mast, and that the mixed pine-hardwood national forests of western and northern Arkansas also hold squirrels. The article could not be identified, so treat this as **[UNVERIFIED]**. Supporting evidence: in a 1980 Mississippi study, streamside zones on bottomland sites held more gray squirrels, averaging 4.08 per hectare (via search summary) — [SEAFWA 1980](https://seafwa.org/journal/1980/squirrel-densities-pine-hardwood-forests-and-streamside-management-zones).
- Park forest (already in the corpus): mesic hardwoods on north slopes, pine–oak–hickory on dry south slopes, sweetgum, sycamore, beech and hornbeam in valley bottoms — [NPS I&M: HOSP](https://www.nps.gov/im/htln/hosp.htm); [Encyclopedia of Arkansas: Ouachita Mountains](https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/ouachita-mountains-442/) (via `geography-lakes-mountains.md`).

### Behavior

**Caching (scatter-hoarding):**
- Grays: "In the fall they gather these seeds and stash them individually in shallow holes they dig." Because they bury more than they recover, they help reseed the forest — [UA Extension](https://uaex.uada.edu/environment-nature/musings-on-nature-blog/posts/squirrels.aspx).
- Fox squirrels "cache the seeds in middens instead of stashing them individually" — [UA Extension](https://uaex.uada.edu/environment-nature/musings-on-nature-blog/posts/squirrels.aspx). This rests on that one source and is not checked elsewhere. MDC describes both species simply as storing nuts in the ground — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel).
- Many unrecovered nuts germinate, so squirrels "plant" trees — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel); MDC's black walnut page says the same — [MDC: Black walnut](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/black-walnut).
- Gray squirrels are "the only known long-distance disperser" of white oak acorns — [FEIS: *Quercus alba*](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/tree/quealb/all.html) (Tirmenstein 1991).
- Feeding sign: squirrels "often sit on rocks or logs to eat, leaving piles of walnut, hickory, and acorn shells" — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel). AGFC calls these "cut acorns, hickory nuts or other fodder" on the forest floor — [AGFC 2020](https://agfc.com/en/news/2020/09/30/a-little-noise-never-hurt-a-squirrel-hunt).

**Nests: leaf nests (dreys) vs. tree cavities** — all from [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel):
- Leaf nests are usually in the tops of large trees, averaging about **40 ft** up (as low as 10 ft). A twig frame **12–20 in** across holds a bulky pile of leaves, with a hollow of about 6–8 in and a **side entrance**.
- Summer nests are built of green twigs with leaves still attached and are often flimsy. Winter nests use bare twigs with interlaced leaves and are "very substantial." A squirrel can build one in under 12 hours. Nests last 6–10 months, and some last 2–3 years.
- Cavities are often in old white oaks, sycamores and soft maples, where lost limbs rot. The entrance is gnawed to about **3 in**; the inside is about 6 in wide and 14–16 in deep, lined with leaves.
- **Cavities are preferred for winter and for raising young**; leaf nests are backups near food or where cavities are scarce.
- Post oak leaves "are used for nest building by birds, squirrels, and raccoons" — [Silvics: post oak](https://research.fs.usda.gov/silvics/post-oak).
- Many hickories are used as den trees by several squirrel species — [FEIS: *Carya glabra*](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/tree/cargla/all.html).
- Flying squirrels are very social; winter nests holding up to 20 animals "are not uncommon" — [UA Extension](https://uaex.uada.edu/environment-nature/musings-on-nature-blog/posts/squirrels.aspx).

**Chatter, barks and tail-flag alarms:**
- Gray squirrels "(AKA cat squirrels) are known to scold predators from the safety of the treetops"; squirrels "will often bark at any unusual sound," and a hawk call "can incite a chorus of angry squirrel barks"; a "fat fox squirrel" can "wheeze and chatter" — [AGFC 2020 (Chuck Long)](https://agfc.com/en/news/2020/09/30/a-little-noise-never-hurt-a-squirrel-hunt).
- Research on gray squirrels (Thaddeus McRae and Steven Green, University of Miami) names three alarm calls: **kuk** (short bark), **quaa** (longer squeal) and **moan** (whistling sound). Tail signals are the **twitch** (a controlled arc) and the **flag** (arc, figure eight, circle or squiggle). The researchers could tell ground predators from aerial ones by the signals — [EurekAlert (UM release, 2014-10-21)](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/712150). Moans are linked mainly to aerial threats and flags to ground threats (via search summary) — [*Behaviour* paper record](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1163/1568539x-00003194); [ESA 2012 abstract](https://eco.confex.com/eco/2012/webprogram/Paper36651.html).

**Seasons of activity:**
- Breeding: grays mate in late December to early January and again in late May to early July. Gestation is about 45 days; litters arrive in February–March and July–August, with 1–8 young (2–3 most common). Young leave the nest at 6–7 weeks. Fox squirrels breed 10 days to 2 weeks earlier — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel).
- Arkansas: mid- to late December brings intense breeding chases ("bark, and chatter"). The spring rut is usually in mid-May. Only 20–40% of adults breed twice a year. Squirrels wait for daylight before leaving nests and dens in winter. Early in fall they sit still while eating, but later they move constantly — [AGFC 2017 (Randy Zellers)](https://www.agfc.com/news/squirrels-give-good-reason-to-get-stuck-in-a-rut/).
- Spring food: red mulberry; feeding-stained "purple chests and chins" are common. Fall and winter food: acorns and hickory nuts — [AGFC 2024](https://www.agfc.com/news/arkansas-squirrel-hunting-season-open/).
- Squirrel numbers rise and fall with acorn and nut crops — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel). Squirrel numbers depend largely on the previous year's mast (via search summary) — [nwaonline 2020](https://www.nwaonline.com/news/2020/jun/02/squirrels-key-spring-mulberries/).
- Gray squirrels also take sunflower seeds and peanuts at feeders — [UA Extension](https://uaex.uada.edu/environment-nature/musings-on-nature-blog/posts/squirrels.aspx).
- Predators: coyotes, foxes, domestic cats and dogs, bobcats, raccoons, owls, hawks, tree-climbing snakes — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel). Red fox, gray fox, bobcat and coyote are all on the park list — [NPSpecies](https://irmaservices.nps.gov/v3/rest/npspecies/checklist/HOSP/Mammal).

### Flying squirrel note (optional third faction or night cameo)

- AGFC calls it "Arkansas's most secretive squirrel"; it is nocturnal and prefers heavily wooded areas near water (via search summary) — [AGFC](https://www.agfc.com/news/arkansass-most-secretive-squirrel-has-stylish-air/). It eats mushrooms, lichens and insects as well as nuts — [UA Extension](https://uaex.uada.edu/environment-nature/musings-on-nature-blog/posts/squirrels.aspx). Flying squirrels eat beech fruit — [MDC: American beech](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/american-beech).

### Notes per tree

- **Acorn biology split.** White and post oak acorns ripen in one season and sprout in fall soon after dropping. The red-oak group (northern red, southern red, black, blackjack) takes two years and sprouts the next spring — [Silvics: white oak](https://research.fs.usda.gov/silvics/white-oak); [Silvics: post oak](https://research.fs.usda.gov/silvics/post-oak); [Silvics: black oak](https://research.fs.usda.gov/silvics/black-oak). So a red-oak acorn is the "keeper" that lasts through winter **[inferred]**.
- **Boom and bust.** Insects, squirrels, deer, turkeys and others can eat or damage more than 80% of a northern red oak crop in most years and nearly 100% in very poor years — [Silvics: northern red oak](https://research.fs.usda.gov/silvics/northern-red-oak). White oak's good crops come every 4–10 years, with annual yields from 0 to 202,000 acorns per acre — [FEIS](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/tree/quealb/all.html).
- **Ozark chinquapin and the blight.** Chestnut blight was first described at the Bronx Zoo in 1904 and had reached the Ozark chinquapin by 1940 — [UA Extension (Klingaman 2003)](https://uaex.uada.edu/yard-garden/resource-library/plant-week/chinquapin-ozark.aspx). Encyclopedia of Arkansas: the blight, a fungus accidentally introduced from Asia, caused a sharp decline in both native chinquapins — [EOA: Trees](https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/trees-2104/). Logging and blight in the 1950s–60s nearly eliminated it, and very few remain (via search summary) — [EOA: Chinquapin (Independence County)](https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/chinquapin-independence-county-9072/). conflict: blight arrival "by 1940" (UA Extension) vs. "1950s and 1960s" decline (EOA). Both can be true; keep both.
  - **Ozark Chinquapin Foundation:** founded by Missouri State Parks naturalist Steve Bost. It hand-pollinates surviving healthy trees and avoids hybrids. As of 2019 it ran 16 test plots with about 1,000 seedlings and saplings, plus 45 healthy wild trees. A detached-leaf assay shows blight resistance in about five days, and some trees tested more resistant than Chinese chestnut. Surviving large trees are mostly in Missouri and Arkansas, and the foundation keeps their locations confidential — [NatGeo (Robert Langellier, 2019-06-24)](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/environment/article/saving-chestnut-trees-ozarks). Former tree height about 60 ft **[inferred]** (test saplings of about 30 ft are "half the height of a full-grown tree").
  - Taxonomy conflict: *Castanea ozarkensis* (NPSpecies, NatGeo) vs. *C. pumila* var. *ozarkensis* (UA Extension, EOA).
- **Osage-orange names.** "Bodark" is a worn-down form of French *bois d'arc* ("wood of the bow"), from the Osage practice of making bows from it (via search summary) — [EOA: Bodark](https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/bodark-4366/). The Arkansas Tree Database lists Osage Orange, Bois D'arc, Bodark and Hedge Apple and calls it native to Arkansas — [Arkansas Tree Database](https://lrsotree.uada.edu/gallery/osage_orange_bois_darc_bodark_hedge_apple). Before settlement its natural range covered only about 10,000 sq mi — [Silvics](https://research.fs.usda.gov/silvics/osage-orange). **[inferred]** Garland County drains to the Ouachita River, not the Red River, so park trees are more likely planted or escaped than relict native.
- **Sweetgum** "is found throughout Arkansas" but was "probably introduced" to the Ozark and Ouachita mountains after European settlement; gumballs are "golf ball-sized" — [UA Extension (Klingaman 2000)](https://uaex.uada.edu/yard-garden/resource-library/plant-week/sweetgum.aspx).
- **Pine-and-oak forest:** shortleaf pine "is a dominant species in large areas of the Interior Highlands"; upland forests on acidic rock were mainly shortleaf pine–oak–hickory; Arkansas has 32 kinds of oaks (31 of them trees) — [EOA: Trees](https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/trees-2104/).

## 4. Prevailing wind at Hot Springs (Memorial Field, station HOT)

**Method:** 85,121 hourly routine observations from the Hot Springs Memorial Field ASOS (HOT), covering 2016-01-01 to 2025-12-31 UTC, were downloaded from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive — [IEM ASOS download](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/request/download.phtml?network=AR_ASOS); [IEM wind rose for HOT](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/sites/windrose.phtml?station=HOT&network=AR_ASOS). |---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Summer (Jun–Aug) | 4.3 mph | 30% | **E 18%**, S 10%, SSW 9% | 7% | 0.3% |
| Fall (Sep–Nov) | 4.3 mph | 32% | **E 22%**, ENE 10%, S 8% | 8% | 0.4% |
| Year | 4.9 mph | 26% | **E 19%**, S 9%, ENE 8% | — | — |

- Windiest month: **March** (6.1 mph mean); calmest: **September** (4.1 mph, 34% calm). Highest sustained reading in the decade: 31 mph (spring).
- Daily cycle (year, local standard time approx.): about 3.3 mph from midnight to dawn, rising to about 7.2 mph from 12–2 p.m., then easing after 5 p.m.
- Second directions shift by season: **northwest** in winter (cold fronts **[inferred]**), **south/south-southwest** in spring and summer (Gulf flow **[inferred]**).
- **[inferred]** The strong easterly share probably reflects the airport's local terrain (valley channeling) and may not match ridgetop winds on Hot Springs or West Mountain. No ridge-top wind data was found.
- The search found no published prevailing-direction table for KHOT. ## 5. Squirrel lore, local stories and notable facts

- **White squirrels nearby:** Marionville, Missouri, calls itself "The Home of the White Squirrel"; towns with white squirrels (albino and/or white-furred gray variants) attract tourists — [MDC](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-gray-squirrel-and-eastern-fox-squirrel). Marionville is roughly 175 mi north of Hot Springs **[inferred]** (est. great-circle). Olney, Illinois, also has a white squirrel population, first seen in 1902 and protected by local ordinance (via search summary) — [Roadside America](https://www.roadsideamerica.com/story/2666). No Arkansas white squirrel colony was found. A single albino was found in a Jonesboro cemetery in 2023, and scientists estimate about one in 100,000 squirrels is born albino (via search summary) — [ABC7 / AP](https://abc7chicago.com/albino-squirrel-rarity-arkansas-rare-squirrels/13895255/).
- **Tame squirrels behind the Row:** a 1978 magazine piece describes a shaded brick promenade behind Bathhouse Row where visitors could talk to and feed tame squirrels, chipmunks and birds (via search summary; page returned 403) — [D Magazine, Feb. 1978](https://www.dmagazine.com/publications/d-magazine/1978/february/hot-springs-genteel-soul-bringsem-back/). This is the only Hot Springs-specific squirrel reference found. Treat it as a single period description **[UNVERIFIED]**; it is the best real anchor for squirrels around the Grand Promenade.
- **"Cat squirrel":** Arkansas hunters' name for the gray squirrel — [AGFC 2020](https://agfc.com/en/news/2020/09/30/a-little-noise-never-hurt-a-squirrel-hunt).
- **Black walnut race:** the USDA Forest Service says people "must be quick to harvest them before the squirrels" — [Silvics: black walnut](https://research.fs.usda.gov/silvics/black-walnut).
- **Chinquapin place name:** the community of Chinquapin (Independence County) is named for the trees (via search summary) — [EOA](https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/chinquapin-independence-county-9072/).
- **[LEGEND]** A blog guesses that "Bodark" evolved into "Ozarks," and its author says this cannot be proven (via search summary) — [The Ozark Tree blog](https://ozarkbill.com/tag/trees/). Do not present it as fact.
- No Hot Springs squirrel legend, ghost story or tourism tale was found.

## Game hooks

All **[inferred]** design ideas built on the facts above.

1. **Factions by habitat.** The Gray Clan (Hot Springs Mountain) is nimble and uses dense cover: smaller, quicker, and their silver tail flag reads well against dark canopy. The Fox Clan (West Mountain) is heavier and holds the open ridges: stronger throws, slower reloads. Size ratio about 1 : 1.5 by weight (MDC means).
2. **Nut ammo set with real traits** (sizes from MDC):
   - Hazelnut: tiny, fast, early-season (July–Aug).
   - Post or southern red oak acorn: ½–¾ in, standard.
   - White oak acorn: ¾–1 in, "sprouts on landing" gag, since white oak acorns germinate in fall.
   - Northern red oak acorn: 1–1¼ in, heavy.
   - Black hickory or pignut: hard shell, bounces.
   - Mockernut: up to 3.5 in, "notoriously hard to open," the heavy cannonball.
   - Black walnut in green husk: messy splat, with a dark stain decal.
   - Pecan: long, spins in flight.
   - Sweetgum gumball: spiky; sticks to roofs; flagged in-game as "not a nut."
   - Osage-orange: rare, huge (4–5 in, 2+ lb), slow arc, splits into seeds.
   - Pine cone: shreds into seeds.
   - Ozark chinquapin: golden spiny bur, rare "restoration" collectible.
3. **Mast-year mechanic.** Each season rolls a "mast year": white oak booms only every 4–10 years, red oaks every 2–5, hickories every 1–3. A boom fills the ammo supply; a bust forces players to dig up caches.
4. **Caching as resource banking.** Grays bury nuts one at a time in many holes and forget some, which then sprout into new trees on the map over seasons. The Fox Clan keeps a midden pile (single-source behavior, so call it game flavor).
5. **Season clock** (month by month):
   - Jul–Aug: hazelnuts.
   - Sep–Oct: main barrage.
   - Late Oct–mid Nov: color peak and pine seed.
   - Dec: rut chases (cartoon "zoomies") and the first cold-front northwest wind.
   - Jan–Apr: cache-only rations, then redbud and dogwood bloom.
   - May: mulberry "purple chin" power-up.
6. **Nest types as bases.** Leaf drey: quick to rebuild, under 12 hours, so it respawns, but it is fragile. Cavity den in an old white oak: armored, and preferred in winter.
7. **Alarm UI.** A "kuk" bark ping marks incoming nuts; a "moan" whistle warns of a hawk (aerial hazard); a tail-flag emote signals a ground threat such as a fox or bobcat.
8. **Wind mechanic.** Light winds (0–15 mph scale), calm about a quarter of rounds. Bias the draw toward **east** wind, which pushes Gray Clan throws west across the valley as a tailwind and is a headwind for the Fox Clan. Spring rounds are windiest; afternoon rounds are about twice as windy as dawn. Add winter northwest gusts and summer south breezes as alternates.
9. **Canopy palettes per season** from the table in section 3. Pine stays `#2F4A2A` on the south-facing bands all year.
10. **Rare skins:** black-morph gray, white (a nod to Marionville, labeled as a Missouri town) and masked fox squirrel (labeled "rare variant").
11. **Educational end-cards:** "Squirrels plant forests," "Hedge apples and bois d'arc bows," "The chinquapin comeback," "Why gumballs aren't nuts."

## Caveats

- **Species split by mountain is fiction.** No source assigns grays to Hot Springs Mountain or fox squirrels to West Mountain. The NPS lists the fox squirrel only as **Probably Present**, and iNaturalist has just 5 research-grade Garland County records (68 for grays).
- **Most nut sizes and months come from Missouri (MDC) and range-wide USDA texts**, not Arkansas or Garland County measurements. Expect local timing to vary by a week or two **[inferred]**.
- **Size conflicts:** UA Extension and MDC give different length and weight ranges; both are kept.
- **Fox squirrel middens:** a single source (UA Extension); MDC describes generic in-ground storage. Treat as flavor.
- **Masked or black-faced fox squirrels** are documented in South Carolina and Mississippi, not Arkansas **[UNVERIFIED]** locally.
- **Fall peak** rests on search summaries of Arkansas.com (page now 404) and 5NEWS; one tracker says about Nov 9 for the park. Drought years can mute color entirely (2019).
- **Dogwood and redbud bloom in Hot Springs:** no dated local record was found; the March–April window is **[inferred]** from range-wide Silvics months and a UA Extension garden note (via search summary).
- **Wind:** the statistics were computed by this session from IEM's ASOS archive, not taken from published NOAA normals. They reflect the airport site (about 3 mi southwest of downtown per the corpus), not the ridges. The easterly dominance may be local terrain. Calm hours lower the means.
- **Ozark chinquapin:** taxonomy conflict (*C. ozarkensis* vs. *C. pumila* var. *ozarkensis*); blight timing conflict (by 1940 vs. 1950s–60s decline); no nut-drop months sourced; squirrel use not named. Foundation founding year not found; the foundation's own site (ozarkchinquapin.com) redirected and was not read.
- **Osage-orange in the park** is Probably Present and likely planted or escaped **[inferred]**; Garland County lies outside its Red River native range.
- **"Garland's oak"** (*Q.* × *garlandensis*) is in the NPSpecies list; its naming origin was not researched.
- **Hunting:** the season dates come from an AGFC WMA page for 2026–27. Hunting and the World Champion Squirrel Cook-Off (Springdale, per [AGFC 2024](https://www.agfc.com/news/arkansas-squirrel-hunting-season-open/)) are context only and should not appear in a wholesome game. Park hunting rules were not checked.
- **Lore:** the tame-squirrel promenade (D Magazine 1978) is a search summary of a page that returned 403. No Hot Springs squirrel legend was found. The Bodark-to-Ozarks idea is **[LEGEND]** or speculation.
- **All hex values are estimates** matched to written descriptions; none were measured.
- iNaturalist and NPSpecies counts were pulled 2026-11-01 and will change.
